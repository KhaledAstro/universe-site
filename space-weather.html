Physics, signatures & data sources

Types of space weather events

What each one physically is, how you recognise it in data, and exactly where the data lives. A companion table to the Sun–to–Earth guide.

On the names. Three labels in common infographics are not catalogue terms, so searching for them returns nothing useful. “Plasma cloud” is the ICME / magnetic cloud — the interplanetary part of a CME. “Radiation belt surge” is a relativistic electron enhancement of the outer Van Allen belt. “Magnetosphere compression” is a sudden impulse and magnetopause inward motion. Each section below is filed under the name the literature and the archives actually use.

1 · Solar flarephotons · 8.3 min

The physics

A flare is the sudden release of energy stored in the non-potential (sheared and twisted) coronal magnetic field of an active region, converted by magnetic reconnection. In the standard CSHKP picture a current sheet forms below the erupting structure, reconnection there accelerates electrons and ions, the electrons stream down the newly reconnected legs, and they slam into the dense chromosphere. The chromosphere cannot radiate that energy away fast enough, so it heats to ~10–30 MK and expands upward — chromospheric evaporation — filling the loops with hot plasma that then shines in soft X-rays. That is why the soft X-ray light curve is the time integral of the hard X-ray one, which is the Neupert effect:

FSXR(t) ∝ ∫0t FHXR(t′) dt′

Total energy budget is 1029–1032 erg, rising to ~1033 erg for the most extreme events. Roughly 10–50 % of that goes into non-thermal electrons above ~20 keV, which is the strongest constraint any flare model has to meet. The GOES class is defined purely by the 1–8 Å peak flux: X1 = 10−4 W m−2, M1 = 10−5, C1 = 10−6, and so on down through B and A.

Two durations matter. Impulsive flares peak in minutes and are usually confined. Long duration events (LDEs) decay over hours and are almost always eruptive, meaning a CME went with them. Your confined homologous events are the interesting middle case: the reconnection happens, the flare is real, but the overlying field holds the rope down.

2 · Coronal mass ejectionplasma · 1–4 days

The physics

A CME is the expulsion of a magnetic flux rope plus its frozen-in plasma from the corona into the heliosphere. Typical mass 1015 g (range 1014–1016), speed 200–3000 km/s, kinetic energy 1029–1032 erg — the same order as the flare it comes with, which is the clue that both are drawing on the same stored magnetic energy.

The launch is a loss of equilibrium. A flux rope sits in balance between the upward hoop force of its own current and the downward tension of the overlying arcade. When the rope is pushed past the critical height, or the overlying field is eroded, the balance breaks. The two standard instabilities are the torus instability, governed by the decay index

n = − d(ln Bext) / d(ln h)   —   eruption when n > ncrit ≈ 1.5

and the kink instability, which triggers when the twist exceeds roughly 2–3 turns. When the overlying field falls off too slowly with height — a strong, low-lying arcade — the rope rises, reconnects, and is caught again. That is the confined eruption, and if the source region rebuilds its rope and does it again from the same place, you have the homologous sequence.

In white light you see the CME by Thomson scattering of photospheric light off the free electrons, so what a coronagraph measures is the electron column density along the line of sight, not the magnetic field. The classic three-part structure is a bright leading front (compressed ambient plasma), a dark cavity (the rope itself, low density and high field), and a bright core (erupted filament material).

3 · Solar wind stream (high-speed stream & CIR)recurs every 27 d

The physics

The slow wind is 300–450 km/s, dense and variable, from the streamer belt. The fast wind is 600–800 km/s, tenuous, hot in protons and remarkably steady, and it comes out of coronal holes along open field lines. The Sun rotates, so a coronal hole sprays fast wind into a spiral that runs into slow wind emitted earlier from further west. Where they meet you get a corotating interaction region.

Inside a CIR the plasma is compressed: density and field magnitude both go up, temperature rises, and a forward–reverse wave pair forms at the edges, steepening into shocks beyond ~2 AU (so at Earth you usually see the compression but not yet a clean shock pair). The stream interface separates the dense slow plasma from the hot fast plasma, and shows up as a sharp density drop with a simultaneous temperature jump.

Because the coronal hole survives many rotations, the whole thing comes back at the 27-day synodic period. This is the single most predictable thing in space weather and it is why recurrent moderate storms dominate the declining phase of the solar cycle.

4 · Geomagnetic stormring current · 1–5 days

The physics

A storm is the energisation of the ring current. The chain is: southward interplanetary Bz reconnects with the Earth's northward dayside field at the magnetopause, open flux is dragged over the poles into the tail, it reconnects again in the tail, and plasma sheet ions are injected earthward. Once inside, those 10–200 keV ions gradient-curvature drift westward around the Earth at L ~ 2–7, forming a westward current ring whose magnetic field opposes the Earth's at the surface. So the horizontal field at low-latitude stations drops. That drop, averaged over four stations, is Dst.

The energy content of the ring current and the field depression are tied by the Dessler–Parker–Sckopke relation:

ΔB / B0 = − 2Ering / (3 B02 RE3 / μ0)

The driving and decay are usually modelled with the Burton equation:

dDst*/dt = Q(Ey) − Dst*/τ

where Dst* is Dst corrected for solar wind pressure on the magnetopause currents, Dst* = Dst − b√Pdyn + c; Q is the injection term, roughly proportional to the rectified electric field V·Bs (Bs = |Bz| when southward, else 0); and τ is the decay time, 5–20 h, set by charge exchange with the geocorona, Coulomb collisions and wave scattering. This single equation is why VBz is the best single predictor of storm strength — far better than flare class or CME speed.

Phases: sudden commencement (pressure jump, positive spike), initial phase (Dst slightly positive, minutes to hours), main phase (Dst falls, 2–12 h), recovery (exponential, 1–5 days, often with a fast then a slow component). Intensity classes by minimum Dst: moderate −50 to −100 nT, intense < −100 nT, superstorm < −250 nT.

5 · Coronal holesource, not an event

The physics

A coronal hole is a region where the magnetic field is open — one polarity, field lines that do not close back to the Sun within the corona but connect to the heliosphere. Plasma escapes freely along them, so the density drops by a factor of 2–5 relative to quiet corona and the temperature is lower (~1 MK against 1.5–2 MK). Less plasma and lower temperature means less emission measure in the hot EUV lines, which is why the region looks dark. It is not a hole in anything — it is a drain.

Holes come in three flavours: polar holes, large and long-lived, dominating at solar minimum; equatorial or low-latitude holes, which are the geoeffective ones because their wind can hit Earth; and transient holes (coronal dimmings), which are the evacuated footpoints left behind by a CME and last only hours.

The observable that matters for forecasting is the hole's area and its position relative to the central meridian. Empirically, the arriving wind speed scales with area, and the transit is 2–4 days. The standard physical framework relating open flux to speed is the Wang–Sheeley–Arge model, built on the inverse relation between the flux tube expansion factor and the terminal speed:

fs = (R⊙/Rss)2 · B(R⊙)/B(Rss)   —   larger fs ⇒ slower wind

6 · Aurora (discrete arc)90–400 km altitude

The physics

Aurora is what you see when magnetospheric electrons precipitate into the upper atmosphere, collide with neutrals, and the excited atoms and molecules radiate as they come back down. A discrete arc is the bright, structured, curtain-like form, and it comes from electrons accelerated to 1–10 keV by a field-aligned potential drop at 5000–15000 km altitude, above the upward field-aligned current region — the inverted-V structure. Diffuse aurora is the broad glow from electrons scattered into the loss cone by chorus and ECH waves, with no field-aligned acceleration needed.

The colours are pure atomic physics and they tell you the altitude directly:

  • 557.7 nm green — O(1S → 1D), lifetime 0.7 s, emitted at 100–150 km. The dominant colour because that lifetime is short enough to beat collisional quenching at that density.
  • 630.0 / 636.4 nm red — O(1D → 3P), lifetime ~110 s, so it only survives above ~200 km where collisions are rare. Red tops mean soft (low-energy) electrons stopping high.
  • 427.8 nm blue-violet — N2+ first negative band. Prompt, so it marks the instantaneous precipitation, and needs energetic electrons reaching below 100 km.
  • Lower red fringe — N2 first positive, below 100 km, only in very hard precipitation.

Higher electron energy means deeper penetration, so the colour profile is an energy diagnostic. The oval itself expands equatorward as the storm deepens; at Dst ≈ −100 nT it can reach ~55° magnetic latitude, and in superstorms below 45°.

7 · Plasma cloud = ICME / magnetic cloudmeasured at L1

The physics

The ICME is the CME after it has left the coronagraph field and become an interplanetary structure. It is the same flux rope, now expanded to 0.2–0.4 AU across by the time it reaches Earth, and still carrying the magnetic topology it was born with. This is the object that actually causes the storm, because it is the only thing that can hold a strong, steady southward Bz for ten hours at a stretch. Turbulent fields cannot; a rotating rope can.

A fast ICME drives a forward shock ahead of it, and between the shock and the rope sits the sheath — compressed, heated, turbulent ambient plasma. The sheath and the rope are two separate storm drivers with different characters: sheath storms are irregular and driven by compressed fluctuating fields, rope storms are smooth and deep.

A magnetic cloud is the subset of ICMEs meeting the three Burlaga criteria: field magnitude above ambient, smooth rotation of the field direction through a large angle over ~a day, and proton temperature and plasma beta both low. Only about a third of ICMEs at 1 AU qualify — the rest are ejecta where the crossing missed the rope axis or the structure is too disturbed.

Deceleration between Sun and Earth is aerodynamic drag on the ambient wind:

dv/dt = − γ (v − w) |v − w|

with w the ambient wind speed and γ ~ 10−8–10−7 km−1. Fast CMEs are dragged down toward the ambient speed, slow ones are pushed up. That is why a 2000 km/s launch arrives at 800 km/s, and why Vsky and VICME are never the same number.

Reconstruction methods

  • MVA (minimum variance analysis) — eigen-decompose the field covariance matrix over the cloud interval; the minimum-variance eigenvector is the rope axis normal. Trust it only when the intermediate-to-minimum eigenvalue ratio is > 2–3.
  • Lundquist fit — force-free constant-α solution, BA = B0J0(αr), BT = B0HJ1(αr). Gives axis orientation, impact parameter, handedness and flux.
  • Grad–Shafranov reconstruction — recovers the 2D cross-section without assuming force-free. Needs a good de Hoffmann–Teller frame.
  • FRED — combines the GCS 3D geometry with the in-situ field to predict B0. Comparing the predicted and observed axial field is a genuine end-to-end consistency check on your whole analysis.

8 · Radiation belt surge (relativistic electron enhancement)L* ~ 3–6

The physics

The outer Van Allen belt holds electrons from hundreds of keV to several MeV, trapped on drift shells. Each particle obeys three adiabatic invariants — gyration, bounce, and drift around the Earth — and every acceleration or loss mechanism works by breaking one of them.

A storm does something counter-intuitive to the belt. First it empties it: within hours of the shock the flux at all energies drops by one to three orders of magnitude. This dropout is part real loss (magnetopause shadowing as the boundary is pushed inside the drift shells, plus outward radial diffusion, plus scattering into the atmosphere by EMIC waves) and part reversible adiabatic response to the inflated field — the Dst effect. Then, over the next one to three days, it refills, often above the pre-storm level. Two mechanisms compete:

  • Local acceleration by whistler-mode chorus waves, which break the first invariant through Doppler-shifted cyclotron resonance and pump seed electrons up to MeV energies in place. Its fingerprint is a growing peak in phase space density at fixed μ and K located at L* ~ 4–5.
  • Radial diffusion by ULF Pc5 waves, which break the third invariant and transport electrons inward from an external source. Its fingerprint is a PSD profile that increases monotonically outward with no interior peak.

Plotting radial PSD profiles at fixed μ and K is how you tell those two apart, and it is the cleanest piece of analysis in the whole belt field. Practical consequence: >2 MeV electrons penetrate spacecraft shielding and build up charge in dielectrics until it discharges — deep dielectric charging, the cause of a long list of satellite anomalies. Separately, in very rare huge SEP events, protons can be trapped to form a brand-new inner belt (March 1991 is the textbook case).

9 · Magnetosphere compression (sudden impulse)seconds to minutes

The physics

The magnetopause sits where the solar wind dynamic pressure balances the Earth's magnetic pressure. The Chapman–Ferraro balance, including the factor ~2 from the image current that doubles the field just inside the boundary, gives the standoff distance:

ρv2 ≈ (2BE)2/2μ0  ⇒  rmp ∝ (ρv2)−1/6

That −1/6 exponent is the whole story. The boundary is stiff: to halve the standoff distance you need 64 times the pressure. Nominal standoff is 10–11 RE. Under a strong shock it moves to 6–8 RE, and in the most extreme events it crosses geosynchronous orbit at 6.6 RE, which leaves GEO satellites sitting in shocked magnetosheath plasma with the field pointing the wrong way. Southward Bz makes it worse, because dayside reconnection erodes flux from the subsolar region — this is why the Shue model has both pressure and Bz terms.

On the ground, the compression appears as a sudden impulse (SI): the compressed magnetopause current increases, and the horizontal field at low latitudes jumps upward by a few nT to over 100 nT within minutes. If a storm follows, the same jump is called a storm sudden commencement (SSC) — same physics, different name depending on what happens next. The compression also energises trapped particles adiabatically (conserving the first invariant) and can launch a global poloidal ULF ringing of the whole magnetosphere.

How to use this in one sitting

For any event, walk the chain forward and make each step confirm the previous one:

  • Source — which AR or filament channel? HMI magnetogram, AIA 171/193. Was there a coronal hole nearby that could deflect things?
  • Trigger — flare class and timing from GOES; ribbons in 1600 Å; filament behaviour in 304 Å. Confined or eruptive?
  • Eruption — CDAW entry, dimming, EUV wave, post-eruption arcade. If you have STEREO, run GCS for the true speed.
  • Shock — type II burst, metric and DH. Absence in a well-observed band is a real physical result, not a gap.
  • Particles — GOES protons crossing 10 pfu; CDAW SEP catalogue for the association.
  • Arrival — Richardson & Cane for shock and ejecta times; OMNI for the crossing; MVA and Lundquist for the rope.
  • Response — SYM-H for the SI, Dst for the storm, AE for substorms, GOES electrons for the belt, all-sky or SSUSI for the aurora.

If any link in that chain fails to line up in time, the association is wrong. That is the most common mistake in event papers, and the easiest one to avoid.
